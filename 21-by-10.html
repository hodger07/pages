Why This Matters Now

The first thing I thought about when I sat down to write this was a simple question: why is this a problem with 20-something techs when it wasn't a problem when we had 50? We had no special process back then. No program. No framework. The only answer I could find was one word — management. So I asked myself to define what that word actually meant in two different eras.

Version A  ·  2003 – 2019

Presence was the system. I was there every day, engaged heavily when problems appeared, and I took them head on. I managed liberally — I was the furthest thing from a micro-manager — but I micro-managed behaviors, attitudes, and patterns that were destructive to the team, the customer, and the company. Nobody was immune: accounting, dispatch, CX, the field team, and myself.

I wasn't big on formal 1:1s or award ceremonies. We invested in people every day by paying attention to them as human beings — calling them out when they did something great, and when they didn't. That made them feel seen and cared for. Awards are fine, but without the daily investment they're like flowers on Mother's Day when the rest of the year you don't even notice her. It just doesn't work.

The managers who got the best results were process-driven. When you're managing a lot of techs, you have to have a system — or it doesn't scale, and we've proven that. The best managers ran their own system that was better than the informal collective system techs build to oppose growth. They watched the small things and called them out fast. They didn't get lulled into therapy sessions or the traps manipulative techs will set. They didn't let techs get too big for their own pants.

Version B  ·  2019 – 2025

I checked out. I hit my 50s. I needed a break. The franchisor didn't honor our agreement — that was the first breach for me mentally, and it crushed me. I brought someone in at the top and handed off everything — culture, mindset, the daily standard. That was the mistake. You can't delegate those things. I will never do that again.

Then my father had a near-death experience in late 2020. Our son passed in 2022. My niece in 2024. Jessica's diagnosis in 2025. Bottom line: I have not been fully present, not by a long shot.

The management layer that followed is a more nuanced picture, and I want to get it right. The capability to run this business at a high level exists on this team — I've seen proof of it. What broke down wasn't talent. It was conditions. Managing techs the way Version A required — watching behaviors in real time, calling out the small things fast, being physically present on the floor — does not work through Zoom, Rilla reviews, or from outside the market. You can't hold the line on what you can't see. What we got wasn't bad management by design. It was capable people in the wrong structure — relationships without real-time accountability, presence without proximity. Techs felt the gap. The standard drifted. The admin side followed: remote, scattered, reactive.

The bottom line on Version B: my presence that defined Version A simply wasn't there — and the structure I left behind couldn't replace it. That's the whole story.

I've been approached more times than I care to count to sell this business — the kind of offers where your kids don't have to work. I've turned every one of them down. I say that not to impress anyone, but because it's the most tangible proof I have — for myself and for you — that I'm not finished. I have more gas in the tank and more impact to make in this industry.

"I've had to become acquainted with grief. I've had to figure out its place in my life now, for good. And I've had to figure out what my new 'why' is. In Version A it came from a broken place — a lot of 'have to's' and 'must do's.' That worked for the first 30 years. It doesn't motivate a man with money and accomplishments over 50. So Version B of myself is simple: I do this going forward because I choose to. Because I like to build things. Because I enjoy developing people. Because I love the trades. I want to chase the perfect lap. And the perfect lap is not the fastest lap."

My Priorities — In Order

  • 1
    Jessica. Everything else loses to this. Every meeting, every fire, every $20K job. She comes first because she's leaving and the business isn't.
  • 2
    My sobriety and System B practice. Forty years of sobriety and growing a business at a CAGR of 45% for over a decade has taught me one thing better than anything else — how to hold a daily standard through whatever life throws at it. That same discipline is what's going to keep me clear-headed and useful over the next stretch. You're going to get a version of me that's deliberate, direct, and present in the time I'm with you. Not reactive. Not scattered. That's the commitment I'm making to myself so I can then make it to you.
  • 3
    3–4 hours per day dedicated to this business. That time runs on one operating rhythm: 21 by 10. Five functions. Twenty-one metrics. Cleared by 10 AM. Every day. If we can clear all 21 by 10, the business runs. If we can't, it doesn't. It's that simple.

The hard part isn't the list. The hard part is leaning into the items that go red — full stop. That's where you truly separate the drivers from the passengers. Who leans in to fix the reds versus who gives glowing speeches full of empty word salads? Who has the hard conversation with the perpetrator the moment they see it — instead of making excuses internally and letting it drift into the abyss?

In this company, you don't get to watch something go wrong and keep your hands clean. If you saw it, you owned it the moment you didn't say or do anything. Letting something stay red without naming it is the same as making it red yourself.

The list makes the red visible. The team makes the red green. That's the muscle we're rebuilding. The work starts now.

The Operating Framework

21 Metrics Across Five Core Functions

Each function below carries a single diagnostic question. If the answer is "no," one of the metrics beneath it needs your attention. Green or red — know where each location stands by 10 AM, who owns it, why it's red, and what the same-day plan is. That's cleared.

01
Function One

Demand

Are we getting enough of the right calls at the right cost?

Demand is the engine. Without consistent, qualified call volume at a defensible cost, nothing downstream matters. A healthy demand function means we're visible, trusted, and converting attention into booked appointments — without overpaying to do it. Marketing volume and lead cost are the inputs; phone conversion and repeat business are the proof that we're executing on both ends.

Metric 01

Marketing Volume

Enough calls coming in. Consistent inbound volume that gives the team real opportunities to close and serve every day.

Metric 02

Cost Per Lead

Not overspending on leads. Acquisition cost tracked against revenue to protect margin before the job even starts.

Metric 03

Repeat Customers

Keeping customers happy enough to come back. Loyalty is our lowest-cost lead source — protect it at all costs.

Metric 04

Phone Conversion

Booking the calls that come in at all costs. Turning rings into appointments before opportunities walk out the door.

02
Function Two

Conversion

Are we turning calls into completed, profitable work?

A booked call is a starting point, not a win. Conversion tracks everything between a confirmed appointment and a completed, profitable job. Speed, dispatch intelligence, reliability, and close rate are the variables between a full truck and a wasted run. This is where strong field leadership separates good operations from great ones — and where Version A management consistently won.

Metric 05

Speed to Call

Getting out before they cancel. Time between booking and arrival is one of the most controllable cancel-rate factors we have.

Metric 06

Dispatch for Profit

Right tech, right call. Strategic dispatch decisions that maximize close probability and average ticket simultaneously.

Metric 07

On-Time Arrival

Showing up when we said we would. Reliability is table stakes and a direct predictor of both close rate and customer satisfaction.

Metric 08

Close Rate

Closing the call. The tech's ability to present options, build trust on-site, and convert a visit into a committed job.

03
Function Three

Delivery

Are we executing the work and getting paid?

Delivery is the core promise — we showed up, did the work, handled any problems, and got paid. Simple in theory, consistently where operations break down in practice. The sequence matters: complete the job to standard, resolve any issues before collection, then collect. A weak delivery function erodes everything upstream — reputation, repeat business, and team morale.

Metric 09

Do the Work

Completing the job to standard. The tech leaves with the problem solved — not just documented, not partially addressed.

Metric 10

Customer Issues

Handling problems before we collect. Fast, accountable resolution during and after the call — before a complaint becomes a review, becomes a reputation.

Metric 11

Collect for the Work

Getting paid for what we did. Same-day collection discipline after the job is right — protects cash flow and eliminates AR drag.

04
Function Four

People

Are our techs and team holding the line?

Operations doesn't break in systems — it breaks in people. This function tracks the human behaviors that either hold the standard or gradually erode it. The Version A managers who got the best results were process-driven, watched the small things, called them out fast, and didn't let techs get too big for their own pants. That's the model. A strong people function means managers are managing outcomes, not personalities.

Metric 12

Tech Behavior

Show-up time, call-outs, and system manipulation. The baseline conduct that builds or destroys trust with dispatch and management.

Metric 13

Tech Refusals

Handling techs who say they won't run another call. A direct challenge to leadership authority — addressed in the moment, not managed around.

Metric 14

Tech Control

Techs taking over their schedule, customers, and time. The slow drift toward independence that undermines dispatch, pricing, and brand integrity.

Metric 15

Dispatcher Line-Holding

Dispatchers who hold the standard with techs. The frontline enforcement that prevents problems from ever escalating to management.

Metric 16

Recruiting & Training

Bringing in and developing new techs while retaining and training existing ones to top standard. The pipeline that determines whether the team grows stronger or slowly degrades.

05
Function Five

Standard

Are we hitting our numbers and holding our identity?

Standard is what separates a company with good days from one with a durable identity. Revenue targets matter. So does leadership presence after hours, a crisp brand in the field, and staying ahead of tools reshaping the industry. At the center is core values alignment — the non-negotiable that determines how decisions get made when no one is watching. This is what we're building toward and what we're judged by.

Metric 17

Revenue Targets

Hitting the number in every shop every day. If not — score it, understand why, and track it. A missed number without an owner is just a missed number.

Metric 18

Nights & Weekends Coverage

Managers and dispatchers watching CX, dispatch, and calls outside business hours. This is when we lose ground — leadership presence is not optional after hours.

Metric 19

Standards of Care & Image

Training techs to hold the trades and brand image at all times. Appearance, conduct, and communication that reinforce trust at every customer touchpoint.

Metric 20

AI & Technology Evolution

Keeping pace with tools that are changing daily. Staying ahead of the technology curve that separates competitive operators from those left behind.

Metric 21

Core Values Alignment

Living the values all day, every day. The standard that defines who we are when the metrics are met — and especially when they aren't.

The Operating Standard

100% — and Honest

Two things have to be true about how we run the 21, and they're connected.

Rule One — 100% is the standard

Not 90%. Passing in school is 70%. Passing in business is 100%. If you asked what percentage of safe landings you want from a pilot, correct surgeries from a surgeon, actors hitting their lines on a Broadway stage — the answer is 100%. Hitting 100% makes us average. Above 100% makes us good or great. Anything less, somebody is paying the price — the customer, the tech standing next to the one not pulling weight, or the company itself.

Each of the 21 gets a number behind it. What does green actually mean, by shop, by day? That's homework for this week. Without a number, green is a feeling. We're done managing on feelings.

Rule Two — Honest by 10 AM

Green by 10 AM is not the goal. Honest by 10 AM is the goal.

The 21 will have reds every single day. That is the point of having 21 items. By 10 AM, every red has a name on it and a same-day game plan.

Cleared does not mean green.
Cleared means owned.

Carrying a red into tomorrow without escalation is not allowed.

Calling something green that isn’t is grounds for removal.

Honest reds make this business better. Quiet reds are what got us here. If you flag a red and own it, you are doing the job. If you mark something green to make a number or end a meeting, you are the problem we are here to fix. There is no in-between on this one.

In this company, you don't get to watch something go wrong and keep your hands clean. If you saw it, you owned it the moment you didn't say or do anything. Letting a red stay red without naming it is the same as making it red yourself.

The list makes the red visible. The team makes the red green. This is the muscle we're rebuilding.

"Six years of life has come at me, and I've had to figure out what kind of leader I want to be on the other side of it. I'm not the same man who walked the shop every day back in the early 2000s, and I'm not trying to be. But I'm also not done. Not by a long shot. I want to build something more substantial than our 2018 peak of $17M in electrical alone. A year from now this business runs on the rhythm, on the standard, and on a team that holds the line whether I'm here or not. That's the goal. The work starts Monday.

I'm grateful for each of you for being on this with me. Let's go run the perfect lap.
