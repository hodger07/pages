JFI Structural Reality: The Cost of Closing vs. Subsidizing

This analysis does not advocate for keeping Just Fix It (JFI) open. It advocates for making a clear, mathematically grounded decision—and then owning it.

Leadership currently has two legitimate options. The first is to close JFI, which eliminates the division's net loss but requires the three remaining branches (Mister Sparky Houston, Spring, and DFW) to absorb approximately $1.1 million in shared overhead that JFI currently carries. The second is to subsidize JFI through its turnaround—which requires absorbing a projected worst-case 2026 net loss of $210,000 while management executes a defined recovery plan with measurable milestones.

Rich Hodge is aligned with either path, provided it represents management consensus. What is not defensible is the current state: an unresolved loss that drains focus without driving accountability. The absence of a decision is itself a decision—one that produces neither focus nor results.

The purpose of this document is to put the real numbers on the table so that management can reach a genuine consensus, commit to it, and execute against it with clarity. Once the math is visible and the choice is made, there is nothing left to debate—only work to do.

Option 1: Shut JFI Down

If you close JFI, the $1.12 million in overhead expenses is dumped equally onto the three Sparky branches.

$374,750

This is the massive cost that gets added to the middle of EACH Sparky branch's P&L.

*Disclaimer: While closing the division would trigger some marginal cost-cutting, it will not be anywhere close to enough to reduce this $1.12M overhead to zero. The vast majority of this structural cost remains and must be absorbed.

Option 2: Keep JFI Open (Subsidize the 2026 Loss)

If you keep JFI open, JFI uses its own revenue to pay for the vast majority of that $1.12 million overhead. Factoring in the Q1 collapse and projecting a continued bleed through July, the projected worst-case net loss for 2026 is ~$210,000. The three Sparky branches just split that loss.

$70,000

This is the worst-case cost EACH Sparky branch pays to cover JFI's shortfall while it rebuilds.

The Four Brothers Analogy (Simplified Math)

Imagine you and your three brothers share a massive apartment that costs $1,120 a month in rent. Your fourth brother, JFI, currently pays the whole rent bill using money from his part-time job.

But JFI's job doesn't quite cover everything. Based on his worst-case 2026 projections, he is going to be short by $210 every month. He asks the three of you to chip in $70 each to cover the difference while he gets a better job.

You get annoyed and say, "JFI is broke! Kick him out!"

If you kick JFI out, his part-time job money leaves with him. But the landlord still wants the $1,120 rent. Now, you and your other two brothers have to split the entire bill. Instead of paying $70 each to help JFI out, you are now paying $374 each.

The Conclusion: Leadership must decide which reality they want to manage. Do we shut JFI down and hold the three Sparky branches accountable for absorbing the $1.1 million in displaced overhead? Or do we keep JFI open, hold the Sparky branches accountable for subsidizing the projected $210k shortfall, and hold JFI accountable to a turnaround timeline? The math is clear. Now the decision must be.
