JFI Structural Reality: The Cost of Closing vs. Subsidizing

This analysis does not advocate for keeping Just Fix It (JFI) open. It advocates for making a clear, mathematically grounded decision—and then owning it.

Leadership currently has two legitimate options. The first is to close JFI, which eliminates the division's net loss but requires the three remaining branches (Mister Sparky Houston, Spring, and DFW) to absorb approximately $1.1 million in shared overhead that JFI currently carries. The second is to subsidize JFI at its current net loss of $147,000 per year while management works a defined turnaround plan with measurable milestones.

Rich Hodge is aligned with either path, provided it represents management consensus. What is not defensible is the current state: an unresolved loss that drains focus without driving accountability. The absence of a decision is itself a decision—one that produces neither focus nor results.

The purpose of this document is to put the real numbers on the table so that management can reach a genuine consensus, commit to it, and execute against it with clarity. Once the math is visible and the choice is made, there is nothing left to debate—only work to do.

Option 1: Shut JFI Down

If you close JFI, the $1.12 million in overhead expenses is dumped equally onto the three Sparky branches.

If you keep JFI open, JFI uses its own revenue to pay for almost all of that $1.12 million overhead. It comes up slightly short, resulting in a net loss of $147,476.98. The three Sparky branches just split that loss.

The High School Roommate Analogy

Imagine you and your three brothers share a massive apartment that costs $1,120 a month in rent. Your fourth brother, JFI, currently pays the whole rent bill using money from his part-time job.

But JFI's job doesn't quite cover everything—he is short by $147 every month. He asks the three of you to chip in about $49 each to cover the difference.

You get annoyed and say, "JFI is broke! Kick him out!"

If you kick JFI out, his part-time job money leaves with him. But the landlord still wants the $1,120 rent. Now, you and your other two brothers have to split the entire bill. Instead of paying $49 each to help JFI out, you are now paying $374 each.

The Conclusion: Leadership must decide which reality they want to manage. Do we shut JFI down and hold the three Sparky branches accountable for absorbing the $1.1 million in displaced overhead? Or do we keep JFI open, hold the Sparky branches accountable for subsidizing the $147k shortfall, and hold JFI accountable to a turnaround timeline? The math is clear. Now the decision must be.

The 80/20: Two Line Items, One Problem

Why the 80/20 Rule? When a P&L bleeds, weak management wastes time arguing over coffee budgets or shaving 2% off fifty minor line items. That lacks focus and lets the real culprits hide in the spreadsheet. The Pareto Principle kills the noise. It proves that out of all expenses, just two behavioral line items vaporized nearly $150,000 in 90 days. This removes the ability to blame "the market" or "overhead" and shrinks the turnaround plan into exactly two accountable targets.

Combined Q1 ad spend and discounts total $148,168. On a revenue base of $381,817, that means 38.8 cents of every dollar walked out the door before a single technician got paid, before rent, before parts.

Apply the Pareto Principle and the picture sharpens: these two controllable line items account for the overwhelming majority of the cash destruction. Fixed overhead didn't spike. Payroll didn't balloon. The furnace that's supposed to generate customers is burning cash at the same rate while producing half the output—and the pricing discipline that's supposed to protect margin is giving it away on the front end.

Ad Spend Accountability: $76,570 across Q1 while sales fell 49%. The spend held flat at ~$25K/month even as January's $168K in revenue collapsed to March's $86K. No throttle adjustment. No pivot. No kill switch. A marketing engine running on autopilot while the results disappear is not a strategy—it is an unattended furnace burning cash. Management must answer why the exact same spend in March yielded half the revenue it did in January.
Discount Accountability: $71,598 surrendered—18.7% of gross revenue over 90 days. January alone gave back nearly $30K. When you are losing money on the bottom line, every unearned "Manager Pricing Exception" is a direct deduction from the survival runway of the company. Management must identify exactly who is authorizing these exceptions and halt them immediately.

The Conclusion: The structural fixed overhead is not what broke the Q1 P&L. A total lack of management accountability on pricing discipline and marketing efficiency drove this collapse. A turnaround plan does not need 40 steps. It needs immediate, aggressive correction on these two lines.
