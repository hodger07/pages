Predictive Index Analysis

Profile Type: Scholar — Accurate, reserved, imaginative. Seeks high technical expertise. Formal, disciplined, sincere, methodical.

Where He Fits:

  • Technical execution — Data-driven, analytical, organized follow-through. This is why Albert says 90%. Scholars do the work right.
  • Process adherence — Comfortable with strict procedures, rule-follower. Scripting compliance won't be an issue once he learns it.
  • Reflection before action — Takes time to think. In diagnostics, this is a strength.

Where the Gaps Are (the two friction points Rich identified):

Reserved vs. Sociable Target

Significantly below the sociable behavioral target for service techs

Steady vs. Driving Target

Prefers steady pace; misaligns with the urgency a service role demands

Rich nailed it on the call: the Reserved + Steady combo means Justin will be quiet, serious, and cautious with new customers. In a service call, the first 20 minutes are relationship-building — the customer decides if they trust you before you ever open the panel. A tech who takes time to warm up loses that window. The technicals don't matter if the customer never gets comfortable.

Rich's benchmark: either push the social factor up (overcome it with warmth and scripting) or push the driving factor up (overcome it with urgency and confidence). Justin needs to demonstrate one or both in the roleplay.

Interview Highlights

Commission Track Record

Justin transitioned from hourly to commission at Buffalo Electric willingly. Claims 70–80% conversion rate, average tickets of $1,200–$1,500 on circuits and $6,000–$9,000 on panels. Was earning 10% for the sale plus 10% if he installed — 20% total. Nominated for "heavy hitter." This isn't a guy who's afraid of sales; he chose it.

Why He Left Buffalo

Call volume collapsed. Went from 3–4 calls per day to 1 call every 3–4 days. Electrical was only 10% of Buffalo's business (90% AC). He resigned — wasn't fired, wasn't managed out. He left because there wasn't enough work. That's a reasonable exit and actually a good sign: he wants to stay busy.

Same-Day Install Preference

This is a big alignment point. Justin said he preferred selling and installing same day but was never allowed to at Buffalo. ABRH's model is built on this. He's not being asked to do something new — he's being given permission to do what he already wanted.

The Van Conversation

Justin admitted his truck was dirty when he left Buffalo. He didn't lie about it. Rich flagged it hard, and Justin took it straight. The honesty matters more than the dirt — but the dirt still matters.

Schedule / Wife Dynamics

Rich probed deep. Justin's wife doesn't love late hours. Justin's position: he's fine grinding as long as it's not 10pm every night, 6 days a week. Rich tested this with the hunter-gatherer speech. Justin didn't flinch. He set a reasonable boundary without being defensive about it.

Rich's Read

Rich ran three culture tests in this interview. Each one matters.

The Van Speech

"Your van is your leading indicator of how professional you take your career. Nothing pisses me off more than a guy not taking care of his van, not showing up, and not doing what you say you're going to do. I'd rather you tell me your van will look like hammered shit than lie to me about it." — Richard Hodge

This wasn't about the van. It was about standards and honesty. Justin passed the honesty test by admitting it. Now he has to pass the standards test by never letting it happen again.

The Integrity Speech

"People don't quit because they can't make money. They quit because they make a deal and don't keep their end." — Richard Hodge

Rich is telling Justin: we'll hold up our end. You hold up yours. The deal is clear. The expectations are clear. Don't sign up for something you won't do.

The Hunter-Gatherer Speech

Justin's answer was solid: he'll grind, but he has a reasonable limit. Rich can respect a boundary; he can't respect a leash.

Roleplay Scenarios for Zoom Tryout

These are designed to stress-test the exact gaps the PI and interview revealed. Don't softball these.

Scenario 1: The Cold Open

Tests: Reserved factor — first 20 minutes rapport building

Play an older homeowner who's skeptical and doesn't want to chat. See if Justin can warm them up, build trust, and transition to the diagnostic without awkward silence or jumping straight to technical. This is the whole ballgame.

Scenario 2: The Upsell Pivot

Tests: Steady factor — pace and urgency in presenting options

Justin diagnoses a circuit issue ($1,200 fix) but the panel is 30 years old. Can he confidently pivot to the $7,000–$9,000 panel replacement conversation without hesitating or underselling? Does he present it with conviction or does the Steady trait make him tentative?

Scenario 3: The Price Objection

Tests: Objection handling under pressure

"That's way too much. My buddy said he'd do it for half that." See how Justin responds. Does he fold, get defensive, or hold the line with value-based scripting? This is Rich's make-or-break.

Scenario 4: The Spouse Objection

Tests: Closing through delay tactics

"I need to talk to my husband first." This is the most common stall in residential service. Justin needs to handle this without being pushy but without just accepting it. Look for: does he ask the right follow-up questions? Does he create urgency?

Scenario 5: The Late Call

Tests: Schedule flexibility / attitude

It's 5:30 PM and dispatch sends a call 40 minutes away. How does Justin react? Enthusiasm? Reluctance? This tests the schedule boundary in real-time.
