1. Marriage Market Asymmetry

This is not flattery. This is arithmetic.

Start with every American male under 30. Filter for physical apex — tall, attractive, fit. Filter for UHNW family with generational equity architecture. Filter for a 4-year state-regulated trade license — wealthy kids do not pull wire in Texas attics in August; they don't sit through apprenticeship when they don't have to. Filter for Eagle Scout with Congressional Record recognition at 17. Filter for active IFR-rated pilot. Filter for five years of deliberate succession mentorship by a high-net-worth father — not inheritance, but a son being intentionally prepared to carry a serious operation forward.

Each filter compounds. By the third, you're under ten. By the fifth, you're under one.

There is exactly 1 man under 30 in the United States with this combination of physical baseline, Eagle Scout credential, licensed trade, instrument rating, generational wealth architecture, and deliberate leadership preparation. The number is not approximate. It is 1.

Now run it on the female side. Start with 11 million American women aged 23–27. Filter for attractive and fit, credentialed healthcare career, emotionally grounded, genuinely athletic, four years single by choice rather than settling, values-aligned and actually available. Each filter cuts hard. Abby lands at roughly 300–400 women nationally — about 1 in 30,000 in her age cohort. Genuinely rare. Not for what sounds impressive on paper, but for the combination that actually matters: attractive PA, competitive athlete, emotionally organized, available, substance over flash.

She is not a 1-of-1. There are other women in her tier. There is no other man in his.

The ratio is 400-to-1. For every Kasey that exists — and there's roughly one — there are three to four hundred Abbys. She's not sitting across from a good option. She's sitting across from a statistical anomaly.

Abby doesn't do that math consciously. She doesn't think in ratios. But four years of being single by choice means four years of watching what walks through the door, and her nervous system has already done the calculation her mind hasn't. She chose empty over settling — which means she can feel the difference when something real shows up.

That feeling doesn't land as confidence. It lands as the question her whole operating system was built to ask: do I deserve this?

The asymmetry doesn't make her secure — it makes her grip harder, move faster, declare on Date 3 what most people won't say in six months, offer to follow on Date 4, and ask why he's touching her like she matters when she's already decided she might not be enough. The rarer he is, the louder that question gets.

She's not playing a market. She's trying to earn a seat at a table she doesn't realize was built for her.

The Downside Case

Do not read this as pessimism. Read it as the cost of getting this wrong.

If she never crosses into full, uncontrolled emotional vulnerability — if the chosen openings are all she ever offers — here is what that marriage looks like:

You get a wife who is loyal, competent, and beautiful. Who cooks. Who shows up. Who handles everything with grace and never quite lands. The home runs. The events get attended. The right things get said at the right times. And you will feel something missing and not be able to name it. Not because it is subtle. Because it is specific: the distance isn't between you and her. It is between her and herself. She will be managing her presentation inside the marriage the same way she has managed it across five dates — choosing which walls to lower, timing every opening, never letting you see the version of her that is not impressive, not composed, and not useful.

That is not intimacy. That is a performance of intimacy. And the difference between those two things is the difference between a marriage and a well-built house nobody fully lives in.

The loneliness of that specific arrangement is worse than obvious loneliness. With obvious loneliness you can name it, address it, change it. With a performance of intimacy, you will feel the distance and have no language for it. Over time you will adjust your expectations downward. You will tell yourself this is just what marriage is. The gap between what you expected and what you have will calcify into "normal." And "normal" is the most dangerous word in the English language when it is doing the work of covering something that is not okay.

She will exhaust herself, too. Performing intimacy is harder than living inside it. She will give and give and give and never feel fully received — because what she is giving is not fully herself. She will grow resentful of the gap she created and call it his failure to see her. He will feel the distance and call it her reluctance to need him. Both will be right. Neither will have the vocabulary to say it.

That is not a good marriage. That is a performance of a marriage. The question is not whether she is capable of more. She is. The crossing is hers — she has to be the one who goes unmanaged, uncontrolled, actually exposed. Nobody can do that for her. But she will only cross if the other side feels safe enough that her system stops blocking the door. Creating those conditions is his job. She crosses first. He receives. Those are different responsibilities, and neither works without the other.

The downside is a marriage where no one is home. The upside is two people who know exactly what an empty house sounds like — and built this one on purpose.
